    How to Protect Your Pipes Before the Next New Hampshire Freeze

    By Andrew Blake · October 6, 2026 · 5 min read

    How Much Freeze Risk You Actually Have

    Water expands about nine percent when it freezes. The ice plug itself is seldom where the pipe splits. Pressure builds between the ice and a closed faucet, and the pipe gives way in that trapped stretch, which is why burst lines often show up a few feet from the coldest spot and only start spraying once things thaw. A single cold night can do it.

    Pipes in exterior walls, crawlspaces, camps and vacation properties are especially likely to freeze when the heat is turned down.

    Pipe in an unheated crawlspace, an exterior wall, an attic or a garage is at the most risk, and so is anything on a wall that takes the wind head on. Those areas can sit well below freezing while the living room reads 68. That is where the work begins.

    Outdoor Plumbing Comes First

    Disconnect every garden hose before the first freeze. A hose left on a spigot traps water in the valve body and splits it. Frost-free sillcocks have a long stem that shuts the water off inside the heated wall, but they only work if the hose comes off so the barrel can drain. Older spigots need the inside shutoff closed and the outside valve left open.

    Sprinkler systems need their own shutdown. Shut the main irrigation valve, then crack the test cocks on the backflow device so it drains. Lines buried below the frost line usually come through fine, but the backflow assembly sits above ground, and a cracked brass body on a pressure vacuum breaker is one of the most common spring repair calls we get. In long-winter areas, blowing the lines out with compressed air is cheap insurance.

    Protecting Pipes Inside the House

    Foam pipe sleeves are cheap and work. Buy the size that matches the pipe, usually 1/2-inch or 3/4-inch. Slide them over every exposed run in the crawlspace and garage, tape the seams, and pay extra attention to elbows and tees, since a gap at a fitting leaves the most vulnerable part of the line bare. For the worst runs, add listed heat cable under the insulation.

    On the coldest nights, open the kitchen and bathroom cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls so house heat reaches the pipes. Leave a thin drip running on both handles of a faucet on an outside wall. Moving water is slow to freeze, and an open tap relieves the pressure that actually bursts the pipe. Keep the thermostat at 55 or higher even if you leave town. Keep the garage door shut.

    Crawlspace vents and gaps around pipe penetrations let cold air blow straight onto the plumbing. Close foundation vents for the winter if your home has them. Seal holes where pipes pass through rim joists or exterior walls with expanding foam or caulk, because a pencil-sized gap with wind behind it can freeze a pipe inside an otherwise warm wall. That one is easy to miss.

    Find your main shutoff before winter. There is usually a valve where the line comes into the house, plus a curb stop at the meter. Turn it once a year so it does not seize, and if it is an old gate valve that will not close all the way, have it swapped for a quarter-turn ball valve, which takes a plumber about an hour on most homes. A stuck shutoff turns a small burst into a soaked floor.

    Water heaters in a garage or crawlspace need attention too. Insulate the supply and outlet pipes for a few feet above the heater. A heater that sits in an unheated space and loses power or gas during a cold snap can freeze in its own lines, so know how to shut off the cold supply and open a hot tap to relieve pressure if that happens. Leave the tank full unless the room will stay below freezing for days.

    When to Call a Professional

    No water from a faucet on a freezing morning means a line is frozen somewhere upstream. Leave the faucet open so melting water has somewhere to go. Warm the suspect section slowly with a hair dryer or a space heater kept well back, and never use an open flame, because a torch on a frozen copper line can start a fire inside the wall or split the pipe from steam. Shut the main right away if the frozen spot is buried in a wall or a ceiling starts to drip, then reach FVR Plumbing at (844) 442-1569. A thawed line still needs a pressure check for splits before the water goes back on.

    Fall Inspections Worth Scheduling

    A fall walkthrough takes about an hour. The tech checks exposed runs, hose bibs, the water heater and the main shutoff. Spots that froze in past winters get a closer look, and that is often the moment to talk about rerouting a line out of an exterior wall or replacing an old spigot with a frost-free model long enough to reach heated space. A fall repair costs less than a midwinter emergency.
