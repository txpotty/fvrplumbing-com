    Hard Water in New Hampshire: When a Water Softener Pays for Itself

    By Maria Delgado · September 27, 2026 · 5 min read

    What Hard Water Means in New Hampshire

    Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium picked up from rock and soil, and labs measure it in grains per gallon. Water above roughly 7 grains per gallon counts as hard. Above 10 grains, you will see white crust on faucets and spots on glasses within weeks of cleaning. A cheap test strip gives a rough number.

    A large share of homes rely on private wells, and some wells carry naturally occurring arsenic, radon or iron that call for treatment.

    Your water provider posts a yearly quality report that lists hardness, often in milligrams per liter. Divide that number by 17.1 to get grains per gallon. Wells vary more. On a well, pay for a lab test, since iron and manganese decide which equipment will work.

    What Hard Water Costs You

    The water heater takes the worst of it. In a gas tank, scale piles up on the bottom and blocks heat from the burner. That popping or rumbling during a heating cycle is the scale, and it means more fuel for the same hot water. Electric elements crust over and burn out early.

    Scale also works on everything else that touches water. Single-handle faucet cartridges get stiff and begin dripping. Inlet screens on the dishwasher and washer plug up, and in a few years a showerhead can lose half its spray pattern to scale. You also use more soap.

    • Water heater: shorter life and higher fuel use
    • Faucets and valves: worn cartridges and drips
    • Tankless units: scale in the heat exchanger and error codes
    • Laundry: stiff towels and more detergent

    When a Softener Pays for Itself

    Run the numbers on your own house. On hard water a tank heater might give you 8 years, and on softened water closer to 12. Add the soap savings and fewer faucet repairs. For a family of four on water above 10 grains, most softeners we install cover their cost in five to seven years.

    Tankless water heaters change the math. On hard water, most makers call for a yearly descale, and some will deny a warranty claim for scale damage. A softener can stretch that to every few years. Your warranty papers may list a hardness cutoff, usually near 11 or 12 grains.

    Picking the Right Softener

    Size the softener by grains removed per day, not by the box. Multiply your hardness by the gallons your household uses each day, which is about 75 gallons per person. A family of four at 12 grains needs to remove about 3,600 grains a day, so a 32,000 or 40,000 grain unit fits most homes. Oversized units waste salt.

    • Metered regeneration: regenerates based on gallons used and uses less salt
    • Timer regeneration: cheaper, but regenerates on a schedule even when it is not needed
    • Salt-free conditioners: reduce scale buildup but do not remove hardness

    Salt-free conditioners get a lot of advertising. They change the way the minerals behave so less scale sticks to pipes and heaters, but the calcium is still sitting in the water. Glasses will still spot, and your test strip will show the same number. Above about 15 grains, only an ion-exchange softener takes the hardness out.

    Getting a Softener Installed

    A softener needs a drain for the brine cycle and a bypass valve, so you can take it offline without shutting off the house. Most installs go near the main line in the garage and take about half a day. Outdoor hose bibs are often left on hard water, since there is no point softening lawn water. Ask for a hardness test first.
