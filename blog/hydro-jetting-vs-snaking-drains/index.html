    Hydro Jetting vs. Snaking: Which Drain Cleaning Method Do You Need?

    By Aaron Mitchell · August 22, 2026 · 6 min read

    What Is Actually in the Pipe

    Before we pick a tool, we want to know what the clog is made of. A sudden stop after wipes go down is not the same job as a kitchen drain that has been getting slower for months. Roots and grease respond to different methods. The history tells us a lot.

    Rocky glacial soil and shallow ledge are common in New Hampshire, which can make digging for sewer and water lines slow and expensive.

    Ground movement opens joints, and open joints let roots in. A root mass in a sewer line is one of the most common reasons a cable machine comes back out wrapped in fibers. It also tells us a simple clearing will not last. Roots regrow fast.

    Drain Snaking: The Standard First Step

    A drain snake, also called an auger or a cable machine, is a long coiled steel cable with a cutting head on the end. It goes into the line, a motor turns it, and the cutter chews through the blockage. Most calls take under an hour. Sink and tub lines get a small handheld machine with quarter-inch or 3/8 cable. On a main line we use a bigger unit with 1/2 or 3/4 inch cable and a cutter sized to the pipe.

    • Good for: hair, wipes, a single hard blockage, light roots
    • Limits: opens a channel but leaves grease and scale on the pipe walls
    • Price: usually the lower-cost option

    The weakness is that a snake opens a hole but does not clean the pipe. A 4-inch line coated in grease might get a 2-inch channel through the middle, which flows fine for a few weeks. After that, the leftover grease grabs more debris and it clogs again. Repeat calls are the sign.

    Hydro Jetting: Cleaning the Whole Pipe

    A hydro jetter pushes water through a hose at 3,000 to 4,000 psi on a typical residential line. Jets on the back of the nozzle drive it up the line and scrub the full circumference of the pipe as it travels. It cuts grease and sludge off the walls. Roots get sliced off flush with the joint.

    Water in much of New Hampshire is soft and often slightly acidic, which can slowly corrode copper pipe and cause pinhole leaks.

    Scale and grease are where jetting really earns its cost. Inside cast iron, mineral scale narrows the bore and gives grease a rough surface to grab. A descaling nozzle on the jetter brings the pipe back near its original size, so it stays clear for a year or longer, not a few weeks. Commercial kitchens jet on a schedule.

    • Good for: grease, sludge, scale, recurring roots
    • Limits: needs a camera check first on old or fragile pipe

    Choosing Between Them

    A first-time clog in a single fixture almost always gets a snake. It is faster and costs less, and usually that is all it takes. A line that clogs again within a few months, or one that a cable cannot get through, gets a camera and then usually a jetter. The camera decides.

    Newer homes mostly rely on copper or PEX supply lines with plastic drains, which hold up better against corrosive water.

    Pipe condition limits the choice. Jetting at full pressure inside a cracked clay line or thin, rusted cast iron can make a bad pipe worse. With an older line, we put a camera in first and either jet at reduced pressure or stick to a cable if the pipe is fragile. PVC and newer cast iron handle full pressure fine.

    Booking the Right Service

    Tell the dispatcher which drains are slow and how long it has been happening. That one detail usually tells us which truck to send.
